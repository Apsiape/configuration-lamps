\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

Amenable groups are the infinite groups closest to finite ones. Every finite piece of an amenable group can be imitated by permutations of a
finite set, with products correct on all but a small fraction of the points. How many points does such an imitation need? For a finite piece
$E$ of a group, a \emph{chunk}, Cornulier's \emph{sofic profile} $\sigma_E(r)$ is the least number of points on which $E$ can be modelled with
multiplication correct, and distinct elements separated, on all but a fraction $1/r$ of the points~\cite{Cornulier2013}. He asked whether a
sofic group can have a profile that is unbounded and not linear in $r$~\cite[Problem~3.18]{Cornulier2013}.

In earlier work we found such a profile in an amenable group. Houghton's group $H_3$ is elementary amenable and finitely
presented~\cite{Brown1987}, with an explicit presentation due to Johnson~\cite{Johnson1999}, and one of its chunks has profile at least
$\exp(\Omega(r^{0.129}))$~\cite{DouglasMghirbiA}. To our knowledge it was the first amenable group shown to have superpolynomial profile. This contradicts
Cornulier's extension theorem~\cite[Theorem~3.15]{Cornulier2013}, which would bound that profile by $O(r^2)$, and the conclusion drawn from
it in~\cite[Example~3.16]{Cornulier2013}, that every elementary amenable group has polynomial profile; Section~7 of~\cite{DouglasMghirbiA}
shows where the argument for that theorem breaks. The
proof is a counting criterion (Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}). If a few relations force many displaced copies of $S_3$ to commute cheaply, every
model must be large, because nearly commuting copies of $S_3$ carry entropy. The criterion can never give more than $\exp(O(r))$. This paper
shows that an amenable group can come close to that ceiling.

\begin{theoremA}
There is a finitely presented elementary amenable group $\Gamma$, generated by five elements, with a chunk $E$ for which the following hold.
Put $\kappa=6+\log107<12.75$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt]
\item There are absolute constants $c,C>0$ such that for all $r\ge2$
\[
\exp\Bigl(\frac{c\,r}{(\log r)^{\kappa}}\Bigr)\ \le\ \sigma_E(r)\ \le\ \exp(C\,r\log r).
\]
So $\sigma_E(r)=\exp(r^{1+o(1)})$. Every chunk $E'$ of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\sigma_{E'}(r)\le\exp(C_{E'}\,r\log r)$ for all $r\ge2$. The lower
bound is certified by an explicit set of $25$ relations of $\Gamma$.
\item The same relations control unitary models. Two of the generators, $a$ and $c$, give $b=ac^4$, and $a,b$ generate a copy of $S_3$ in
$\Gamma$. Let $0<\Delta\le1$. There are $c_\Delta,e_\Delta>0$ with the following property. Let $\phi$ assign unitaries in $U(D)$ to the five
generators so that each of the $25$ relations is mapped within $e$ of $I$ in the normalized Hilbert--Schmidt norm, where $0<e\le e_\Delta$,
and put $\phi(b)=\phi(a)\phi(c)^4$. If $\|\phi(a)\phi(b)-I\|_2\ge\Delta$, then
\[
\log D\ \ge\ c_\Delta\,\frac{e^{-2}}{(\log(1/e))^{2\kappa}} .
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theoremA}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main-group} gives the precise statement, with the chunk $E$ and the $25$ relations of Table~\ref{tab:certificate}. The lower
bound is within a polylogarithmic factor in the exponent of the most the criterion can give. The exponent $\kappa$ records the price of bringing
two lamps together: about $N^2$ local moves, each paid for by the Dehn function of $H_3$, which grows at most like $n^{4+\log107}$~\cite[Theorem~3.3]{DouglasMghirbiA}. That
polynomial bound is the one input of Theorem~A whose proof we do not reproduce; with Lee's exponential bound alone the method gives only
$\exp(c\,r^{1/(1+B)})$ for a constant $B$ that we cannot make explicit (Remark~\ref{rem:lee}). The $107$ is
the factor by which a doubling endomorphism of $H_3$ multiplies area in~\cite[Section~3]{DouglasMghirbiA}; it is the only constant in $\kappa$
that is not structural, and a quadratic Dehn function for $H_3$ would give $\kappa=4$. The upper bound comes from models that hash
configurations by random affine maps. These are far smaller than the models that Følner sets give~\cite[Proposition~3.14]{Cornulier2013},
because the Følner function of $\Gamma$ is doubly exponential (Proposition~\ref{prop:folner-lower}). The finite presentation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:fp}) comes from Cornulier's criterion for permutational wreath products~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{Cornulier2006}. Finally,
$\Gamma$ embeds in Brin's higher-dimensional Thompson group $3V$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:3V}). So $3V$, a finitely presented simple group whose
soficity is open, has a chunk whose profile is finite and $\exp(r^{1+o(1)})$.

\paragraph{Configuration lamps.} In Houghton's group the copies of $S_3$ sit on the points of a ray. Bringing two of them together costs a
word as long as the distance $k$ between them, and commuting them costs at least of order $k^2$ relations~\cite[Proposition~3.4]{DouglasMghirbiA}.
A family of $N$ copies spreads over distance $N$, so its commutators are expensive. We move the lamps off the points and onto the finitely
supported binary configurations of the three rays of $H_3$. Houghton's group moves the points. Finitely supported affine maps act on the
configurations: a translation flips one point, and a transvection adds one point into another. One copy of $S_3$, a lamp, sits on each
configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:lamps}). The $2^N$ configurations supported on the first $N$ points of a ray are reached by words of length
$O(N^2)$, and two of them differ by a configuration that at most $N$ transvections clear down to a single point. Each step touches at most
three points, so its cost is governed by the Dehn function of $H_3$ at scale $N$, which is polynomial~\cite[Theorem~3.3]{DouglasMghirbiA}.
Commuting two of the $2^N$ lamps therefore costs a polynomial in $N$, which is polylogarithmic in the number of lamps. That is exactly what the
criterion needs.

\noindent Transport by the powers of one element gives exponent $1/(p+1)$ when far commutators have area of order $k^p$. Along a ray of $H_3$
the area is at least quadratic~\cite[Proposition~3.4]{DouglasMghirbiA}, so this transport stops at $1/3$, which the dyadic group $\mathcal G$
attains with seventeen relations. Thompson's group $F$ transports cheaply along exponentially growing orbits, and a finitely presented
subgroup $\mathcal L$ of Thompson's group $V$ turns this into a dichotomy: either $V$ is not sofic, or its models of a fixed chunk need
$2^{c\,r/\log^2r}$ points. Models of $\mathcal L$ exist if $F$ is amenable, and $\mathcal L$ is hyperlinear exactly when $F$ is
(Proposition~\ref{prop:thompson-hyperlinear}). The configuration lamps keep the exponential transport and drop the condition on $F$.

\paragraph{Quantum memory.} The second half of the paper turns $\Gamma$ into a quantum channel. Picture a device that must apply the same
channel $n$ times. Each input arrives, the device acts on it together with its memory, and the output leaves before the next input comes in.
The device may swap memory qubits for fresh ones and may start with some purity, but it never resets, and every qubit it keeps counts. How much
memory does it need? This question goes back to Kretschmann and Werner, who showed that every causal quantum process is a concatenation of
memory channels~\cite{KretschmannWerner2005}, and to Rybár and Ziman, who asked when a device that is never reset applies the same channel to
each of its inputs. They showed that random unitary channels can be applied this way with a fixed finite memory, while nonunital channels
cannot~\cite{RybarZiman2008}. We let the memory grow with the number of uses and ask how fast it must grow. We set up the resource accounting in~\cite{DouglasMghirbiB}. The \emph{exchange} counts the qubits
the device swaps out, and the \emph{purity} counts the qubits it starts with or imports, minus their entropy (an entropy deficit, not $\Tr\rho^2$). The error is
measured against observers who choose their inputs adaptively and keep quantum references, as for quantum strategies and
combs~\cite{GutoskiWatrous2007,Chiribella2009}. A finite set of relations of a
group defines a channel, its \emph{gadget channel}, whose memory cost is governed by how well the group can be imitated: small memory would
give small approximate representations. For a gadget channel in the closure of channels with finite tracial factorizations, the optimal
exchange is $\frac12\log r_*$ qubits per use, where $r_*$ is the Choi rank~\cite[Propositions~3.1 and~9.1]{DouglasMghirbiB}. We call it the
rank rate.

One use reveals nothing: the channel below lives on $\mathbb C^{873}$, and a single round is easy. The dimension $873$ is what the gadget
construction gives for the $29$ words that define the channel; we made no attempt to minimize it. The cost shows up only over many rounds.
From $H_3$ we obtained a channel that needs memory $(n/\log n)^{0.069}$ at logarithmic purity~\cite{DouglasMghirbiB}. The configuration lamps
give nearly linear memory, and a trade-off between memory and purity that is sharp up to polylogarithmic factors. All these bounds are
asymptotic: the polylogarithmic factors have exponent up to $2\kappa+1<26.5$, and the constants, which come from the Dehn function of $H_3$,
are not evaluated, so the lower bounds take effect only for very large $n$.

\begin{theoremB}
The $25$ relations of Theorem~A, with two new letters for $b=ac^4$ and $j=ab$ and four short words that relate them ($29$ words in seven
letters), define an explicit quantum channel $\Phi$ on $\mathbb C^{873}$.
It lies in the closure of channels with finite tracial factorizations, and its Choi rank is $r_*=130$. With $\kappa$ as in
Theorem~A, there are constants $c,c_0,K>0$ and $n_0$ such that the following hold for causal services of $n$ uses of $\Phi$ with error at most
$1/16$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Memory against purity.} If $n\ge n_0$ and $\log n\le B\le c_0n$, every service with purity at most $B$ has memory at least
$c\,n/(B(\log n)^{2\kappa})$, at any exchange. For every $n\ge1$ and $0<B\le n$ there is an exact service, with error $0$, purity less than
$B$ and exchange $(n-1)Q$, whose memory is $Q\le K(n/B)\log(2n/B)$. In particular, services with purity $O(\log n)$ need memory
$n^{1-o(1)}$, while memory $Kn\log n$ suffices at zero purity.
\item \emph{The rank rate.} If $n\ge n_0$, every service with exchange at most $\frac n2\log r_*$ has memory at least
$c\sqrt n/(\log n)^{\kappa}$, whatever its purity. For every $n\ge2$ there is a service with exchange at most $\frac n2\log r_*$ whose memory
and purity are both at most $K\sqrt{n\log n}$.
\end{enumerate}
So memory and purity trade at a fixed rate: in the range of~(a), the least memory at purity $B$, times $B$, is $n^{1+o(1)}$. With exchange at
most the rank rate the least memory is $n^{1/2+o(1)}$, and it is attained with purity of the same order.
\end{theoremB}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main-channel} gives the precise statement, and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} draws it. The engine is a one-round theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:weighted}) for every gadget channel whose relations contain those of $S_3$ and a name for $ab$: a single round of any device
reveals an approximate representation of the group on the device's memory, with relator defect of order the square root of the leakage and
entropy production of that round. In~\cite[Theorem~5.5 and Appendix~B]{DouglasMghirbiB} this was proved for one gadget, built from Houghton's
group. The upper bounds come from sofic models, which give devices directly (Proposition~\ref{prop:sofic-upper}). At most points of a model
the pure bath state reproduces the channel exactly, so a memory that is maximally mixed on those points serves the channel with no error at
all. At the rank rate a device cannot buy memory with purity, because the purity it can spend is at most linear in its memory
(Proposition~\ref{prop:purity-budget}).

\paragraph{Finite environments.} The same bounds say how far from finite the channel itself is. A channel is factorizable if it factors through a finite von Neumann algebra with a faithful trace~\cite{AD2006,HaagerupMusat2011},
and it has a finite tracial factorization if it is $X\mapsto(\mathrm{id}\otimes\tau_M)[U(X\otimes I)U^*]$ for a unitary $U$ on $\mathbb C^d\otimes\mathbb C^M$, that is, if
it can be implemented with a finite bath in its maximally mixed state. By a theorem of Haagerup and Musat, the closure of these channels
consists of the channels that factor through an ultrapower of the hyperfinite $\mathrm{II}_1$ factor, and whether it contains every
factorizable channel, in every dimension, is equivalent to the Connes embedding problem~\cite[Theorems~3.6 and~3.7]{HaagerupMusat2015}.
That problem has a negative answer~\cite{JNVWY2020}, so some factorizable channels lie outside the closure. Inside it, Musat and R{\o}rdam
gave channels that need an infinite-dimensional ancilla~\cite{MusatRordam2020}. Our channel measures how expensive the finite
approximations can be.

\begin{theoremC}
The channel $\Phi$ of Theorem~B is factorizable and lies in the closure of the channels with finite tracial factorizations, and its minimal
Stinespring dilation implements it exactly with a pure environment of dimension $130$. Yet for small $u$, every channel with a finite tracial factorization within
accuracy $u$ of $\Phi$, in Choi or in diamond distance, and every finite mixture of such channels, needs a bath of dimension at least
$\exp\bigl(c\,u^{-1}/(\log(1/u))^{2\kappa}\bigr)$, while dimension $\exp(C\,u^{-1}\log(1/u))$ suffices.
\end{theoremC}

Corollary~\ref{cor:profile} gives the precise statement. Channels in the closure with no finite tracial factorization were
known~\cite{MusatRordam2020}, and for the Houghton channel of~\cite[Theorem~D.1]{DouglasMghirbiB} the exponent of the profile lies between
$0.069$ and $1/3$. What is new here is the quantitative profile: for $\Phi$ the exponent is $1-o(1)$, close to the most the method can
certify.

\paragraph{Physics.} A local lattice system in $\nu$ dimensions whose bath starts maximally mixed produces, in time $T$, only channels that can
be served with memory polynomial in $T$ and $\log n$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:light-cone}). So it needs time at least about $\delta^{-1/\nu}$ to
produce $\Phi$ to accuracy $\delta$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:preparation}). Autonomous dynamics with a bath in its tracial state have long-time
limits that are self-adjoint~\cite[Remark~10.3]{JungeLeMerdyXu2006},~\cite[Remark~5.2]{HaagerupMusat2011}, yet such a limit can still be
expensive: a bounded time-independent Hamiltonian, not local, produces in the long run a channel that trades memory and purity at the same
rate as $\Phi$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:lazy}). Whether local autonomous dynamics can produce an expensive channel is open.
